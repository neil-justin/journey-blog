# 3rd Year — Systems

If second year was about structure, third year at AISAT was about systems — many parts interacting, none of them fully under my control. It was the heaviest academic year I had, and it ended in a way I did not expect.

## Operating Systems: the machine underneath

OS 101 was third year's signature course for me. Processes, scheduling, memory management, concurrency — before this course, the operating system was weather: something that happened to my programs. After it, I understood the machinery.

The idea that changed me most was concurrency. Multiple actors, shared resources, and no global clock — and the realization that most "impossible" bugs are really timing bugs. Years later, working on a multi-tenant platform where many organizations share one system, I kept hearing OS 101's voice: what happens when two things touch the same resource at once? That question has never once been irrelevant.

## Networks and Communications: nothing is alone

NC 101, Networks and Communications, extended the same idea across machines. Layers, protocols, packets — the internet is agreements all the way down, and most of the agreements are older than me.

What networks taught me is that distributed systems fail gracefully on the diagram and chaotically in reality. Latency, partial failure, retries — these were textbook concerns in third year. They became concrete concerns in my first job, when the API that works locally meets a real user on a real connection.

## Application Development and Emerging Technologies: building whole things

CC 106 pulled the year together. Where earlier courses taught components, this one pushed toward complete applications — and toward the honest question of which technologies are worth their complexity. Around this era I built MarkNote, a full MERN-stack note app with Docker in the mix, deliberately practicing the full path: data model, API, frontend, deployment. It is on GitHub, and it was the closest coursework-adjacent thing I made to a real product.

## The year the classroom ended

Third year holds one more distinction, and it is the reason this post ends where it does: halfway through this chapter of my life, my real work life started. While still working through my degree, I joined Career Team LLC as a software engineer — a real company, a real codebase, real users.

Everything I had learned to that point — precision from first year, structure from second, systems from third — got its first genuine test at once. The classroom did not stop being important. It stopped being enough.

That story is the next chapter.
