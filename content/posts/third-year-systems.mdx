Third year was the heaviest academic year I had, and it ended somewhere I didn't expect.

Operating Systems retired a picture I'd carried since first year: the OS as weather, something that just happens to my programs. After OS 101 I could see the machinery — processes, scheduling, memory. The piece that stayed was concurrency: many actors, shared resources, no global clock. We had a lab where two threads incremented a shared counter and the final number came out different every run, and the professor made us explain why before he'd let it go. I have thought about that counter on roughly every production system I've touched since.

Networks made the same move across machines. The internet is agreements all the way down, most of them older than me. Wireshark labs did more for me than any diagram — latency and partial failure stop being textbook words the first time you watch your own packets retry.

Around this era I built MarkNote, a MERN-stack note app with Docker, deliberately practicing the whole path: data model, API, frontend, deployment. Deployment was where it got real. Docker on my laptop (8GB of RAM, no mercy) took two full evenings of reading error messages I mostly didn't understand. It's on GitHub. Not the prettiest thing I've made, but the first one where every layer was mine.

Halfway through this year, still working through the degree, I started my first software engineering job. That's the real boundary of third year: the classroom didn't stop being important. It stopped being enough.

That story is next.
