# Two Educations

I never received one education. I received two, at the same time, from very different institutions — AISAT College and the software industry — and this whole blog has been the story of how they kept interrupting each other. This final reflection is about what each one gave me, what neither could have given alone, and who came out the other side.

## What the degree taught me

The degree gave me the map.

First year taught precision: Computer Programming 1 and Discrete Structures 1 showed me that computing is the art of being exact. Second year gave me structure — Data Structures and Algorithms, Object-Oriented Programming, Automata Theory — the shapes that organized everything after. Third year opened up systems: Operating Systems, Networks and Communications, Application Development and Emerging Technologies — computation at scale, machines interacting with machines. Fourth year is teaching synthesis: Software Engineering, the thesis, the professional practice of the discipline.

No job would have taught me automata. No internship would have handed me a coherent curriculum that builds, course by course, from logic to distributed systems. The degree's gift is breadth with a plan — a map of the entire territory, drawn by people who have thought carefully about what an engineer should eventually know.

But a map is not mileage. For that, I needed the other education.

## What the industry taught me

The jobs gave me the mileage.

Career Team LLC, where I built on a multi-tenant workforce platform in NestJS, Next.js, and MongoDB — thousands of real users, government agencies and school organizations depending on the thing staying up. Thorne Consulting, where I planned features end-to-end, reviewed others' code, managed deployments, mentored two interns, onboarded new engineers, and conducted around 55 technical interviews as the interim lead on a founding team.

Industry taught me what no course can manufacture: consequences. A slow query is a real person waiting. A vague spec is a real colleague's wasted week. An interview is a real decision about a real career, sometimes including mine. Every lesson arrived wearing consequences, and that is why it stuck.

## What neither could teach alone

Here is the sum. The degree gave me knowledge; the jobs gave me judgment. Knowledge says how a system can work. Judgment says what it should cost, whose problem it really is, and whether today is the day to build it — and judgment only comes from shipped work with real stakes.

Each education kept rescuing the other. When my production MongoDB queries crawled, it was second-year data structures that pointed at the answer. When SE 1's process diagrams looked abstract, it was production experience that made them obvious. When I conducted 55 interviews wondering what "ready" means, the answer turned out to be written in both educations' language: reasoning you can explain, limits you admit, judgment under uncertainty — knowledge and mileage, together.

## The journey, compressed

I started at AISAT College unable to write a line of code. Along the way: my first program, my first all-nighter, my first side project on GitHub, my first operating system course, my first production bug, my first spec written for someone else to build, my first intern, my first lead role. Each "first" looked small at the time. Stacked in order, they are the distance between a student and an engineer.

If I compress the whole trip into one lesson, it is this: the two educations are not competing credentials, they are load-bearing walls of the same building. The degree without the work is theory without consequence. The work without the degree is mileage without a map. I got to build both at once, into one career, and I do not think I fully understood how rare that is until writing this reflection.

## Where this ends

This blog closes where the journey currently stands: 4th year, thesis ahead, final subjects in progress, and a working career already running alongside. The horizon in front of me is graduation — but this blog has taught me that horizons are mostly doorways. Wherever I cross next, I am carrying both educations with me, and I know exactly which one taught me to keep asking questions: both of them.

The closer you get to the center, the closer to graduation. I am close now. Thank you for traveling the whole way with me.
