# First Job — Career Team LLC

The professional phase of my journey did not wait for graduation. In May 2025, while still working through my degree, I joined Career Team LLC as a Software Engineer, working remotely for a US company. This post is the story of what that transition actually felt like — not the polished version.

## The shock: real codebases are different species

School assignments have a shape: small, self-contained, written once, graded, discarded. Production code has none of those comforts. My first weeks at Career Team were spent inside a multi-tenant workforce platform — NestJS on the backend, Next.js on the frontend, MongoDB underneath — serving thousands of users across government agencies and school organizations.

Three things hit me immediately.

First, scale is invisible until you inherit it. Every query I wrote touched data belonging to many organizations, not just mine. Multi-tenancy meant every feature carried a question school never asked: whose data is this, and who must never see it?

Second, code is read far more often than it is written. In school, the grader reads your code once. On a 15-person cross-functional team, my code was read by reviewers, extended by teammates, and revisited by me months later at 2 a.m. during an incident. Writing for that audience is a different skill, and school had only hinted at it.

Third, shipped software keeps living. Assignments end. Features do not — they get used, misused, requested, and reworked.

## What I actually did

The work settled into a rhythm of real ownership. I built the multi-tenant admin dashboard and improved its load performance by fixing slow MongoDB queries — the first time my second-year data-structures instincts earned their keep against a genuine production problem. I shipped admin profile management and configurable workforce forms used by client organizations, learning that a "small feature" ripples through schemas, APIs, permissions, and UI all at once.

Beyond the code, I facilitated daily standups for the 15-person team, acting as the bridge between developers and product to keep sprint goals on track. That was unplanned growth: explaining engineering reality to non-engineers, and product priorities to engineers, taught me that the bridge role is where projects actually succeed or fail.

I also conducted technical interviews for engineering candidates and buddied two new engineers through onboarding and codebase walkthroughs. Being the person who explains a system is the fastest way to genuinely understand it.

## What the job gave me

Career Team turned classroom knowledge into working knowledge. Nothing on this platform was conceptually beyond my courses — but everything was beyond my experience. The gap between those two words is what this first job closed.

It also changed how I saw myself. I arrived a student who knew things. I left — in mid-2026 — an engineer whom teams rely on. The next chapter tested that identity harder than I expected, because at Thorne Consulting the reliance became explicit: they asked me to lead.
