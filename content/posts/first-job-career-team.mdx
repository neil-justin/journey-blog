In May 2025, while still working through my degree, I joined Career Team LLC as a software engineer — remote, for a US company. NestJS, Next.js, MongoDB, a multi-tenant workforce platform serving government agencies and school organizations. Three things hit me in the first month.

First, tenancy. In school, my data was my data. Here every query I wrote touched many organizations, and I nearly shipped a dashboard endpoint that returned records across tenants because I forgot a filter on a nested lookup. My reviewer caught it in minutes. I re-read that diff a few times after. School never asked "whose data is this, and who must never see it?" — production asks it about every line.

Second, the audience. In school a grader reads your work once. Here my first PR came back with more review comments than lines of code changed — naming, error handling, one "why does this exist?" I couldn't answer. Embarrassing, then useful. On a 15-person team, writing code other people can extend is a separate skill from writing code that works.

Third, nothing ends. Assignments get submitted and die. Features get used, misused, and reworked, sometimes by me, sometimes by someone who joined after me.

The work settled into real ownership: the multi-tenant admin dashboard (and its slow queries — more on that in the next post), admin profile management, configurable workforce forms for client organizations. I facilitated daily standups, which mostly meant learning to say "blocked" out loud instead of quietly fighting something for two days. I buddied two new engineers through onboarding. Explaining a system, it turns out, is the fastest way to find the parts of it you don't actually understand.
