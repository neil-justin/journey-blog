# 2nd Year — Structure

Second year at AISAT is where the degree changed character. First year taught me to give a computer instructions. Second year taught me to organize chaos — in code, in data, and eventually in my own thinking.

## Data Structures and Algorithms: the year's center of gravity

CC 104, Data Structures and Algorithms, was the most important course of my early years. Arrays, linked lists, stacks, queues, trees, graphs — the material itself is standard. What changed me was the habit it built: before writing anything, ask what the data wants to be.

Almost every performance problem I have fixed since — including slow MongoDB queries in a production dashboard years later — turned out to be a data-structure question in disguise. Who holds this data? How often is it read versus written? What does it need to answer quickly? Second year is where I learned to ask those questions, one assignment at a time.

## Object-Oriented Programming: designing, not just writing

SDF 104, Object-Oriented Programming, was my introduction to the idea that code is an architecture and not just a sequence. Encapsulation, inheritance, polymorphism — the concepts sound abstract until the first time a well-shaped class makes a change trivial that would have been painful otherwise.

In hindsight, OOP was really teaching modularity: put each responsibility somewhere it can change independently. I did not have the vocabulary for that then. I got the vocabulary years later in industry, but the instinct started in this course. When I eventually built MarkNote, a MERN-stack note app, the difference between code I organized and code I merely accumulated was the difference between a project that shipped and one that stalled.

## Automata Theory: the strange beauty of limits

AL 102, Automata Theory and Formal Languages, was the year's most abstract course — state machines, regular languages, grammars. Honestly, it felt the least practical at the time. It grew on me with age.

Automata teaches something humbling: computation has hard edges. Some problems are computable, some are not, and the classes are provable. There is a strange comfort in that. It was also my first experience of theory that is genuinely beautiful — the idea that a machine with a finite number of states can recognize structure in infinite input still strikes me as quietly remarkable.

## Alongside the classroom

Somewhere in these years I built Tasky, a small task-tracking app in plain vanilla JavaScript, and put it on GitHub. No framework, no stack — just the DOM and discipline. It was the first project where I made something for myself rather than for a grade, and it quietly set a pattern: build real things alongside coursework, or the coursework stays theoretical.

## What the year actually taught

Second year gave me structure. Not the syllabus kind — the mental kind. Data structures organized information, OOP organized code, automata organized computation itself. The unifying lesson: complexity is not conquered by working harder, it is conquered by choosing the right shape.

That lesson would be tested hard the following year, when the shapes stopped being homework and started being production systems.
