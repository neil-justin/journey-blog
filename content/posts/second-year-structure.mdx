Second year changed what the work felt like. First year was making the computer do things. Second year was making things stay sane while they grew.

Data Structures and Algorithms did most of it. I lost a weekend to a linked list that kept dropping nodes — the bug was a pointer I reassigned one line too early, and I found it by drawing boxes on paper because I didn't yet trust a debugger. That habit stuck: before writing anything, ask what the data actually is. Who holds it, how often it's read, what it has to answer fast. Years later that question is what fixed a slow dashboard in production. Most performance problems I've met since were data-shape problems wearing a costume.

Object-Oriented Programming was really about where things live. I didn't have that vocabulary yet, just the itch. I had Tasky by then — a small task app in plain vanilla JavaScript, the first thing I made for myself instead of for a grade. Going back over it after OOP, I could see exactly which parts were designed and which parts were accumulated, and only one half of it was still pleasant to change.

Information Assurance and Security 1 was the surprise. I expected hacker-movie material and got threat modeling and access control, which sounded dry until I started my first job and found out it's most of the job.

Automata Theory was the strangest course. State machines, grammars, the halting problem. It felt the least practical of anything I'd taken. I'll admit I mostly enjoyed it anyway — there's something good about a finite machine recognizing structure in infinite input.

One lesson carries the year: working harder doesn't fix a wrong shape. Choosing the right shape does.
