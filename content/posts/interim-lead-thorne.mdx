In March 2026 I joined Thorne Consulting, a US career-readiness startup, as the second engineer on the founding team. By the time my contract ended in September 2026 I was interim lead. The label came after the work did, which is the first thing this chapter taught me.

On a founding team there's nobody to route hard questions to. Being engineer #2 meant my fingerprints ended up on everything: database schemas, API contracts, frontend component breakdowns that other engineers built against, then reviewing what they shipped. I got the schema-for-others thing wrong once — left the notification behavior in a spec undefined because it seemed obvious to me. It wasn't. The engineer built it the other way and it cost about a week to redo. She was kind about it. I've been writing decisions down explicitly ever since, not because specs should be long, but because "obvious" is where rework is born.

The interim lead part was planning, reviewing, unblocking, deciding. I onboarded new engineers on the architecture, which forced me to say out loud choices I'd made by instinct — some of them didn't survive being explained. I mentored two interns. The first intern I had was stuck for two days on something I could've unblocked in ten minutes if I'd asked one more question in standup. I still think about that.

Alongside it, I conducted roughly 55 technical interviews, system design and culture fit. Sitting on that side of the table changed me. I kept grading candidates against a version of me who never pauses, and the people I actually pushed to hire were the ones who thought out loud and said "I'm not sure" when they hit the edge of what they knew. I hold myself to the same standard now, with mixed success.

I left with something no course grants: the experience of being trusted with a team before feeling ready, and finding out that's where readiness actually comes from.
