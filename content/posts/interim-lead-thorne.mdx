# Leading Without a Title — Thorne Consulting

In March 2026 I joined Thorne Consulting, a US career-readiness startup, as the second engineer on the founding team. I arrived as a software engineer. By the time my contract ended in September 2026, I had held the interim lead role — not because anyone handed me a title on day one, but because the work pulled me into it. This post is about what that transition taught me, because leading, it turns out, is mostly invisible before it is official.

## Being the second engineer

Small teams give you the whole picture whether you want it or not. On a founding team there is no dedicated DevOps department, no separate product team, no one else to route hard questions to. Being engineer #2 meant my fingerprints ended up on everything.

The defining work of my time there was planning features end-to-end: specifying database schemas, API contracts, and frontend component breakdowns that mentored engineers built against — then reviewing the code they shipped. This is a different job from writing the code yourself, and I did not expect how different.

When you write code, correctness is between you and the machine. When you write a specification that someone else implements, correctness is between you and a colleague's next two weeks. A vague schema decision becomes someone else's confusing Tuesday. I learned to write specs the way I wished every spec I had ever received had been written: decisions explicit, boundaries named, ambiguity hunted down before it costs someone time.

I also managed deployments and releases, and acted as the bridge between engineering and non-technical staff for day-to-day support. The bridge role — which I first grew into at Career Team — here became half my job. Translation in both directions, daily, with real consequences for being misunderstood.

## The interim lead role

Then the team's structure changed, and I stepped into the interim lead role. The word "interim" taught me something permanent: leadership is not a rank, it is a set of behaviors that become obviously necessary. Planning, reviewing, unblocking, deciding — I had been doing the work before the label arrived to describe it.

The concrete responsibilities compounded. I onboarded new engineers on the codebase's architecture and engineering process, which forced me to articulate decisions I had previously made by instinct. If I could not explain why the architecture was shaped the way it was, either the explanation or the architecture needed fixing.

Two of those mentored engineers were interns I directly mentored — the first time I was responsible not just for systems but for someone else's growth inside one.

## Fifty-five interviews

Alongside all of it, I conducted roughly 55 technical interviews covering system design and culture fit. I expected interviewing to be a duty. It became an education.

Fifty-five candidates is fifty-five mirrors. You watch people reason, bluff, recover, and collapse in real time, and you cannot help calibrating your own habits against what you value in theirs. I developed a concrete answer to a question I had only had theories about: what makes an engineer ready? Not encyclopedic knowledge — reasoning out loud, honesty about the edges of what they know, and judgment under uncertainty.

That question has a personal edge for me. I was a student leading engineers — my own readiness was exactly what I was auditing in others. The interviews made me more honest with myself about it.

## What this chapter taught me

Thorne took the engineer that Career Team built and stress-tested the rest: communication, planning, mentorship, judgment. I left in September 2026 with something no course grants — the experience of being trusted with a team before feeling fully ready, and discovering that readiness is mostly built by exactly that.

The lessons from this whole working era deserve their own post. That is next.
